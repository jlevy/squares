The argument

Atoms, Mass, and the Budget

Place finitely many points in the container and give each a positive weight. Each weighted point is an atom; the mass \mu(R) of a region is the sum of the weights inside it. Every weight is rational, so every mass is a finite exact sum.

Suppose eleven unit squares fit in the side-{{L_DEC}} container. Choose atoms so that both of these hold:

  • every unit square you could possibly place covers mass at least 1;
  • the total mass of all {{N_ATOMS}} atoms is below {{N}}.
\sum_a w_a \;=\; {{TOTAL_TEX}} \;=\; {{TOTAL_DEC}} \;\lt\; {{N}}

The eleven squares are disjoint, so no atom is counted twice and together they cover at least {{N}}. The board holds {{TOTAL_DEC}}. The second condition is a property of the certificate rather than of packing, so the first must fail: eleven such squares do not exist.

The rest of the proof makes the first condition finite to check.

The object

The Atom Set

The certificate holds {{N_ATOMS}} atoms in {{N_ORBITS}} orbits of the container’s eightfold symmetry group \mathbf{D}_4, carrying {{N_WEIGHTS}} distinct weights between {{WEIGHT_MIN}} and {{WEIGHT_MAX}}. That symmetry is condition C0. It lets the proof check angles only up to \pi/4 and reflect every other orientation onto that arc.

The covering condition

Every Placement Covers Mass at Least One

Condition C4 is the claim that every placement covers mass at least 1. A placement has three continuous parameters, two of position and one of angle, so the placements cannot be enumerated. The proof makes them finite twice over: the angle is snapped to a net of {{N_DIRECTIONS}} rational directions, and for each direction the position collapses to a finite grid of cells, because the set of atoms under the square changes only when one crosses an edge.

The figure below evaluates that condition. Every weight is a whole multiple of {{SCALE}}, so the covered mass is an integer count of those units and the readout rounds nothing. The least covered mass, over every placement and all {{N_DIRECTIONS}} directions, is

The angle net

From a Continuum of Angles to {{N_DIRECTIONS}}

The angles cannot all be checked, so {{N_DIRECTIONS}} are checked and the gaps are covered by shrinking. Given a unit square at any angle \varphi, take the nearest net angle \theta and place a slightly smaller square, side B, concentric inside it at angle \theta. If the small square fits, proving the covering property for small squares at net angles proves it for unit squares at every angle. It fits exactly when

B\,(\cos d + \sin d) \;\le\; 1,

where d is the angular mismatch. Since \cos d + \sin d \le 1 + \tan d on [0,\pi/4), it is enough that

B\,(1 + D) \;\lt\; 1, \qquad D \;=\; \max_k \frac{t_{k+1}-t_k}{1+t_k t_{k+1}} \;=\; \max_k \tan\frac{\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k}{2}.

That is condition C3, and it couples the two parameters: a coarser net gives a larger d, hence a smaller B, hence less mass covered, hence a harder C4.

The net stays rational because the angles are carried as half-tangents, \theta_k = 2\arctan t_k, so that

\cos\theta = \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{2t}{1+t^2}

are exact rationals. No angle in the certificate is a floating-point number. The net has to reach \pi/4, and since \tan(\pi/8) = \sqrt{2}-1 is irrational, even that is tested in an exact rational form:

t_K^{\,2} + 2t_K - 1 \;\ge\; 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t_K \;\ge\; \tan\frac{\pi}{8}.
The cost of a coarser net

Why the Net Has {{N_DIRECTIONS}} Directions

The direction count is measurable. Hold the {{N_ATOMS}} atoms fixed, coarsen the net, set B to the largest value C3 then admits, and re-decide C4. Halving the net shrinks B by a quarter of one percent and costs nine percent of the least covered mass.

The close

The Contradiction

Take any packing of eleven unit squares in the side-{{L_DEC}} container. Each square, whatever its angle, contains a side-B square Q_i at one of the {{N_DIRECTIONS}} net angles, which is C3. Because C3 is a strict inequality, each Q_i sits strictly inside its unit square’s interior, so the eleven are disjoint and no atom is counted twice. Each covers mass at least 1, which is C4. Then

{{N}} \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^{{{N}}} \mu(Q_i) \;\le\; \mu\!\left([0,L]^2\right) \;=\; {{TOTAL_TEX}} \;=\; {{TOTAL_DEC}} \;\lt\; {{N}},

which is false. There is no such packing, and s({{N}}) \gt {{L_FRAC}}.

The strict inequality in C3 is what makes the Q_i disjoint. With \le in its place, two shrunken squares could share a boundary atom, the double counting returns, and the sum never reaches {{N}}.

Where the search is

Generator and Verifier

The atoms are not designed; they are solved for. The certificate is a rationalised optimum of a covering linear program,

\tau^*(L,B) \;=\; \min_{w \,\ge\, 0}\; \sum_a w_a \quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{a \in Q} w_a \;\ge\; 1 \;\;\text{ for every placement } Q,

which has one constraint per placement, a continuum, so the rows are generated rather than enumerated, with the same event-cell sweep acting as the separation oracle. A certificate exists exactly when \tau^* \lt n. Note that \tau^* depends on L and B alone and never on n; n enters only in that comparison. A run converging to a round number is therefore a signature of a bug, not a result.

All of that search runs in floating point, because it is a search. None of it is in the proof: the generator emits a file, and the verifier re-decides C0 through C4 on that file in exact rational arithmetic. A wrong linear program yields a rejected certificate, never a false theorem.
