From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Two-dimensional packing problem

**Square packing** is a [packing problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packing_problem "Packing problem") where the objective is to determine how many [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_\(geometry\) "Congruence \(geometry\)") [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square "Square") can be packed into some larger shape, often a square or circle.

## In a square

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Square_packing&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: In a square")]

**Square packing in a square** is the problem of determining the maximum number of [unit squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_square "Unit square") (squares of side length one) that can be packed inside a larger square of side length  a {\displaystyle a} . If  a {\displaystyle a} is an [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer "Integer"), the answer is  a 2 , {\displaystyle a^{2},} but the precise – or even [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic "Asymptotic") – amount of unfilled space for an arbitrary non-integer  a {\displaystyle a} is an open question.[1]

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:5_kvadratoj_en_kvadrato.svg)

5 unit squares in a square of side length  2 + 1 / 2 ≈ 2.707 {\displaystyle 2+1/{\sqrt {2}}\approx 2.707}

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:10_kvadratoj_en_kvadrato.svg)

10 unit squares in a square of side length  3 + 1 / 2 ≈ 3.707 {\displaystyle 3+1/{\sqrt {2}}\approx 3.707}

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Packing_11_unit_squares_in_a_square_with_side_length_3.87708359....svg)

11 unit squares in a square of side length  a ≈ 3.877084 {\displaystyle a\approx 3.877084}

The smallest value of  a {\displaystyle a} that allows the packing of  n {\displaystyle n} unit squares is known when  n {\displaystyle n} is a perfect square (in which case it is  n {\displaystyle {\sqrt {n}}} ), as well as for  n = {\displaystyle n={}} 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15, 24, 34, 35, 46, 47, and 48. For most of these numbers (with the exceptions only of 5 and 10), the packing is the natural one with axis-aligned squares, and  a {\displaystyle a} is  ⌈ n ⌉ {\displaystyle \lceil {\sqrt {n}}\,\rceil } , where  ⌈ ⌉ {\displaystyle \lceil \,\ \rceil } is the [ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function "Ceiling function") (round up) function.[2][3] The figure shows the optimal packings for 5 and 10 squares, the two smallest numbers of squares for which the optimal packing involves tilted squares.[4][5]

The smallest unresolved case is  n = 11 {\displaystyle n=11} . It is known that 11 unit squares cannot be packed in a square of side length less than  2 + 4 5 ≈ 3.789 {\displaystyle \textstyle 2+{\frac {4}{\sqrt {5}}}\approx 3.789} . By contrast, the tightest known packing of 11 squares is inside a square of side length approximately 3.877084 found by [Walter Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Trump "Walter Trump").[4][6]

The smallest case where the best known packing involves squares at three different angles is  n = 17 {\displaystyle n=17} . It was discovered in 1998 by John Bidwell, an undergraduate student at the [University of Hawaiʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hawaiʻi "University of Hawaiʻi"), and has side length  a ≈ 4.6756 {\displaystyle a\approx 4.6756} .[4]

[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mutilated_chessboard_vectorized.svg)A [mutilated chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutilated_chessboard_problem "Mutilated chessboard problem"), an optimal packing for _n_ 2 − 2 squares

Below are the minimum solutions for values up to  n = 16 {\displaystyle n=16} ; the case  n = 11 {\displaystyle n=11} remains unresolved.[7]

Number of unit squares  n {\displaystyle n} | Minimal side length  a {\displaystyle a} of big square  
---|---  
1 | 1  
2, 3, 4 | 2  
5 |  2 + 2 2 ≈ 2.707 … {\displaystyle 2+{\frac {\sqrt {2}}{2}}\approx 2.707\ldots }  
6, 7, 8, 9 | 3  
10 |  3 + 2 2 ≈ 3.707 … {\displaystyle 3+{\frac {\sqrt {2}}{2}}\approx 3.707\ldots }  
11 | 3.877... ?  
12, ..., 16 | 4  
  
Some numbers of unit squares are never the optimal number in a packing. In particular, if a square of size  a × a {\displaystyle a\times a} allows the packing of  n 2 − 2 {\displaystyle n^{2}-2} unit squares, then it must be the case that  a ≥ n {\displaystyle a\geq n} and that a packing of  n 2 {\displaystyle n^{2}} unit squares is also possible.[2]

### Asymptotic results

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Square_packing&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Asymptotic results")]

Unsolved problem in mathematics

What is the asymptotic growth rate of wasted space for square packing in a half-integer square?

[More unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics "List of unsolved problems in mathematics")

For larger values of the side length  a {\displaystyle a} , the exact number of unit squares that can pack an  a × a {\displaystyle a\times a} square remains unknown. It is always possible to pack a  ⌊ a ⌋ × ⌊ a ⌋ {\displaystyle \lfloor a\rfloor \\!\times \\!\lfloor a\rfloor } grid of axis-aligned unit squares, but this may leave a large area, approximately  2 a ( a − ⌊ a ⌋ ) {\displaystyle 2a(a-\lfloor a\rfloor )} , uncovered and wasted.[4] Instead, [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erdős "Paul Erdős") and [Ronald Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Graham "Ronald Graham") showed that for a different packing by tilted unit squares, the wasted space could be significantly reduced to  O ( a 7 / 11 ) ⊆ o ( a 0.637 ) {\textstyle O(a^{7/11})\subseteq o(a^{0.637})} (the latter written in [little o notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_o_notation "Little o notation")).[8] Later, Graham and [Fan Chung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fan_Chung "Fan Chung") further reduced the wasted space to  O ( a 0.631 ) {\textstyle O(a^{0.631})} ,[9] and subsequent work reduced the wasted space to  O ( a 0.625 ) {\textstyle O(a^{0.625})} ,[10] and then  O ( a 0.6 ) {\textstyle O(a^{0.6})} .[11] However, as [Klaus Roth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Roth "Klaus Roth") and [Bob Vaughan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Vaughan "Bob Vaughan") [proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof "Mathematical proof"), all solutions must waste space at least  Ω ( ( a ⋅ | a − round ⁡ a | ) 1 / 2 ) {\displaystyle \Omega {\bigl (}(a\cdot |a-\operatorname {round} a|)^{1/2}{\bigr )}} . In particular, when  a {\displaystyle a} is a [half-integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-integer "Half-integer"), the wasted space is at least proportional to its [square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root "Square root").[12] The precise [asymptotic growth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_analysis "Asymptotic analysis") of the wasted space, even for half-integer side lengths, remains an [open problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_problem "Open problem").[1]

## In a circle

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Square_packing&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: In a circle")]

**Square packing in a circle** is a related problem of packing _n_ unit squares into a [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle") with radius as small as possible. For this problem, good solutions are known for _n_ up to 35. Here are the minimum known solutions for up to  n = 12 {\displaystyle n=12} (although only the cases  n = 1 {\displaystyle n=1} and  n = 2 {\displaystyle n=2} are known to be optimal):[13]

## In other shapes

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Square_packing&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: In other shapes")]

Packing squares into other shapes can have high [computational complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_complexity "Computational complexity"): testing whether a given number of axis-parallel unit squares can fit into a given [polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon "Polygon") is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete "NP-complete"). It remains NP-complete even for a [simple polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_polygon "Simple polygon") (with no holes) that is [orthogonally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_convexity "Orthogonal convexity"), with [axis-parallel sides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectilinear_polygon "Rectilinear polygon"), and with [half-integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-integer "Half-integer") vertex coordinates.[14]
