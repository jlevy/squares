## The Claim and What It Needs

Issue #1038 claims $s(228) \le C$ with $C = 15.596043116632$, the 12-place print of the
certificate’s side $S$, rounded up.
The certificate proves $s(228) \le S$: there are 228 closed unit squares, rotated
freely, with pairwise disjoint interiors inside the closed square $[0, S]^2$. The claim
follows when $C \ge S$, and directly when the same squares are accepted in $[0, C]^2$.

The file is a softschema envelope (`contract: packing.squares:Witness/v2`) around a
Witness/v2 record with `representation: center-basis`, `scalar.kind: rational`,
`origin: lower-left` and `axes: x-right-y-up`: per square a centre $c$ and a basis
$u = (a, b)$, which the schema defines as $[\cos\theta, \sin\theta]$. With $v = (-b, a)$
the square is $\{c + s u + t v : \lvert s\rvert \le 1/2, \lvert t\rvert \le 1/2\}$. The
hypotheses are five:

1. **The reading.** One YAML document with no anchors, aliases, explicit tags,
   directives, duplicate keys, tabs or carriage returns, holding only strings, plain
   decimal integers, mappings and sequences; the envelope above, the witness keys inside
   the schema’s list with every required key present, `square_size: '1'`; the squares
   listed with ids 1 to $n$ in order, each with exactly an id, a centre and a basis of
   two scalars; every scalar a string `p` or `p/q` with no sign but an optional minus,
   no leading zero and $q \ge 1$. Lowest terms are recorded, not required.
2. **The count.** 228 squares.
3. **Unit squares.** Each basis is exactly a unit vector, $a^2 + b^2 = 1$, and then the
   set above is a unit square.
   A basis off the unit circle defines no angle, and both methods refuse it.
4. **Containment.** Each square lies in $[0, S]^2$; the origin is the lower-left corner.
5. **Disjoint interiors.** No two squares’ interiors meet.

A centre and a basis carry no sense of rotation and no choice of corner: the four bases
$\pm u$, $\pm v$ give the same square, and controls G4 and G5 show that neither method
depends on which is written.

`r1038_check.py` decides the first two through the reader, and the next three twice.

- **Method A** (`r1038_sat.py`, Python integers) writes each square’s four scalars over
  their least common denominator $D$, so $c = (X, Y)/D$ and $u = (A, B)/D$ with
  integers. The square is accepted when $A^2 + B^2 = D^2$. Its normals $u$ and $v$ are
  then exactly unit, so on each of the four normals $w$ of a pair the two projections
  are intervals of half-widths
  $h_k(w) = (\lvert u_k \cdot w\rvert + \lvert v_k \cdot w\rvert)/2$ about
  $c_k \cdot w$, and their separation $\lvert d \cdot w\rvert - h_i(w) - h_j(w)$, with
  $d$ the centre difference, is an exact distance, decided over the integer denominator
  $2 D_i^2 D_j$. The margin, the greatest of the four separations, is kept as an exact
  fraction: positive is apart, zero is contact with disjoint interiors, negative is
  overlap. Two convex polygons have disjoint interiors exactly when a line parallel to an
  edge of one of them weakly separates them (the origin then lies outside the interior
  of their Minkowski difference, whose edges are parallel to theirs), so the three signs
  are exactly those three classes.
  A wall’s clearance is the signed distance from the square’s extreme point to it, its
  half-width on an axis being $(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)/2$.
- **Method B** (`r1038_clip.py`, `gmpy2.mpq`) is handed the centres and bases and
  nothing else, and builds each square’s corners $c \pm u/2 \pm v/2$. Four points are a
  unit square in cyclic order when all four sides have squared length 1 and both
  diagonals squared length 2; the shoelace sum, $\pm 2$, gives the sense.
  A pair is decided by clipping one square against the four closed half-planes to the
  left of the other’s counterclockwise edges (Sutherland–Hodgman, keeping points on the
  line), which gives the intersection exactly; it is clipped both ways round, and the
  two results must agree in class and area.
  Empty is apart; non-empty with zero area is contact, since the interior of an
  intersection of convex sets is the intersection of their interiors; positive area is
  overlap, by that area.
  Each wall is classed the same way, by clipping the square against the closed
  half-plane outside it, and containment is decided apart: a unit square lies in the box
  exactly when its intersection with the box has area exactly 1.

The two share only the reader (`r1038_reader.py`), which hands them exact rationals.
The verdict is `ACCEPT` only when both methods accept every square, wall and pair, with
equal classes everywhere; `r1038_decide.py` runs both over every square, wall and pair,
and `r1038_check.py` adds the file’s digest and the claim’s arithmetic.

**F6. The factor’s size.** The issue’s “Certificate” paragraph and the file’s
