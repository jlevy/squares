# How s(5) = 2 + 1/√2 has been proved, and what carries over to R₅ (2026-10-21)

Question: how has the lower bound s(5) ≥ 2 + 1/√2 been proved, and which of those ideas extend to the rectangle
region R₅ = {(a, b) : 5 unit squares fit in a × b}, short side a ≥ 2?  Its boundary there (Friedman, Math Magic June 2010,
pieces 4.3–4.5 of S_4) is b = 3 for 2 ≤ a ≤ 1 + √2, and a + b = 4 + √2 for 1 + √2 ≤ a ≤ 2 + 1/√2 (plan in
`search/rectfront/RECTFRONT.md`, "R_5 — plan").  This note follows `rectangles-small-n-2026-10-20.md`, which covers
general rectangle results and is not repeated here.

## 0. Summary

* **Every proof found is the same idea: a 4-point unavoidable set plus pigeonhole.**  The four points are
  (1, 1), (1, 1 + d), (1 + d, 1), (1 + d, 1 + d), with d = 1/√2.  Every unit square in the container contains one of
  them, so at most 4 squares fit.  The proof splits on where the square's centre lies:
  * in a corner cell: the corner lemma;
  * in a side strip: the side-strip lemma;
  * in the central d × d cell: a central lemma.

  The variants differ only in the central lemma and in how they handle closed versus open containers:
  * Göbel 1979: points pulled in by ε/3, and an inscribed-disc argument in the centre;
  * Friedman DS7: exact points, a triangle lemma, and scaling.

  Neither is case-heavy: Göbel's outline is half a page, and DS7's is three lemmas of about 10 lines each.
* **No other proof of s(5) was found.**  None of the following proves s(5) by another method:
  * Stromquist's memos I–III (1984; memo I §2 credits the n = 5 proof to Göbel);
  * Kearney–Shiu (s(6), s(7));
  * Nagamochi (k² − 2 and k² − 1, which does not include 5);
  * Stromquist 2003 (s(10), s(11));
  * Karakuş.
* **A formalisation exists, contrary to what we assumed.**  chelokot/square-packing-archive proves
  `s5_eq_goebel : IsMinimumSide 5 (2 + √2/2)` in Lean, **lower bound included** (`s5_ge_goebel`, Friedman's 4-point
  set) [read].  Since PR #33 (merged 2026-10-07) it is also in Bend.  The wand125 / our own `lean/` files have only the
  upper bound (`Exact/N5*.lean`) and the trivial `s5_ge_2_leb` (s(5) ≥ 2) [read].  Nothing in Isabelle, Coq/Rocq or
  HOL.
* **On rectangles, the 4-point method reaches part of the boundary, not all of it [I, partly numerical].**
  * Put the points at (1, 1), (a − 1, 1), (1, b − 1), (a − 1, b − 1).  Replace the triangle lemma by a centre-rectangle
    lemma: a centre in an X × Y cell with X + Y ≤ √2 and X, Y ≤ 1 forces a vertex.  This lemma is not in the literature.
    It is observed numerically, and it generalises DS7 Lemma 4.  Its threshold is **exactly** R₅'s two pieces.
  * The side strips are what fail.  Friedman's side lemma needs the gap to be at most 2√2 − 2 ≈ 0.828.
  * With the points allowed to move (Stromquist's corner lemma), the method proves the true boundary:
    * for **2 ≤ a ≤ 3√2 − 2 ≈ 2.243** (the b = 3 piece);
    * for **4 − √2 ≈ 2.586 ≤ a ≤ 2 + 1/√2** (the 4 + √2 piece).
  * The middle stretch, a ∈ (2.243, 2.586), needs something else.  A crude random search found no unavoidable 4-point
    set there (§4).

## 1. Göbel 1979: the original proof [read]

**Citation.** F. Göbel, "Geometrical packing and covering problems", in A. Schrijver (ed.), *Packing and Covering in
Combinatorics*, Mathematical Centre Tracts 106, Mathematisch Centrum, Amsterdam, 1979 (2nd ed. 1982), pp. 179–199.
ISBN 90 6196 180 7.  No DOI.  Full scan of the 1982 second edition, open access at CWI:
https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/12685 (PDF `https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/12685/12685D.pdf`, 62 MB; chapter 12, PDF pp. 187–207).
Earlier notes said there was "no online copy"; that was wrong.

**What it proves.** p. 180: "The exact value of z*(n) is known only for n = 2, 3, 5 and the squares of integers."  The
footnote says Bajmóczy has shown z*(7) = 3, according to Schrijver.  p. 181:

> PROPOSITION 1. S′ := S(2 + ½√2 − ε) cannot be packed with 5 unit squares (ε > 0).

It is presented "to demonstrate a technique for finding non-trivial lower bounds", and the proof is labelled an
"OUTLINE OF PROOF", about 15 lines and one figure.  The chapter says of itself: "proofs are hardly given".

**Argument** (p. 181, Fig. 2).
* Draw four lines inside S′, parallel to the sides at distance 1 − ε/3 from them.  Their four crossings are A, B, C, D.
  The central cell II is then a square of side ½√2 − ε/3.  It suffices that every unit square in S′ covers one of
  A, B, C, D.
* Three cases on the centre M of the unit square:
  1. **M in a corner cell (I):** "an easy calculation in analytic geometry shows that A is covered".  This is the
     corner lemma.
  2. **M in the central cell (II):** if M is closest to A, then d(A, M) < ½, so A is covered.  The unit square contains
     the disc of radius ½ about its centre, and the half-diagonal of II is (½√2 − ε/3)/√2 < ½.
  3. **M in a side cell (III):** without loss of generality a vertex is on the upper edge.  "A simple calculation shows
     that the length of the intersection of S(1) and the line at distance 1 − ε/3 from the upper edge has length
     > ½√2 − ε/3, hence A or B is covered."
* **Gap [I]:** a chord longer than |AB| covers A or B only if the chord overlaps segment AB.  Göbel does not argue that
  the chord sits over AB.  For a tilted square the chord is offset from M's projection.  DS7 Lemma 2 below does this
  properly.
* The ε/3 inset is Göbel's way of handling the boundary: the points are strictly inside, and every covering is strict
  enough for pigeonhole on closed squares to bite.

**Length / case load.** Three cases, one figure.  Two of the cases are "easy calculations" left to the reader.  The
central case is a one-liner (inscribed disc).  This is the simplest known proof.

## 2. Friedman DS7: the textbook version [read]

**Citation.** E. Friedman, "Packing Unit Squares in Squares: A Survey and New Results", *Electronic Journal of
Combinatorics*, Dynamic Survey DS7.
* Versions: v1 1998, revised 2000, 2002, 2005, 2009.
* Current text: https://erich-friedman.github.io/papers/squares/squares.html (read).
* EJC page: https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/view/DS7.
* DOI not checked.

**Statements** (§4 "Technical Lemmas", §5):
* **Lemma 1 (corner).**  A unit square in the first quadrant with centre in [0, 1]² contains (1, 1).
  * Proof: reduce to a square touching both axes at angle θ.  Its far edge crosses x = 1 at
    y = [(1 − sin θ)(1 − cos θ) + sin θ]/sin θ ≥ 1.
* **Lemma 2 (side strip).**  Let 0 < x ≤ 1, 0 < y ≤ 1 and x + 2y < 2√2.  A unit square in the first quadrant with
  centre in [1, 1 + x] × [0, y] contains (1, y) or (1 + x, y).
  * Proof: the lowest corner's height is y − f(θ), with f(θ) = cos θ + sin θ − x sin θ cos θ.  The global minimum of f
    is at θ = 45°.
  * **The condition is sharp:** a 45° square with a vertex on the wall, centred between the points, slips between
    them once x + 2y > 2√2.
* **Lemma 3 (triangle).**  If the centre is in a triangle with all sides ≤ 1, the square contains a vertex.
  * Proof: the diagonals of the square cut the plane into four closed quadrants.  The vertices meet two opposite
    quadrants, and two vertices at distance ≤ 1 cannot both avoid the square.
* **Lemma 4.**  A centre in [0, 1] × [0, 0.4] forces a vertex of that rectangle (used for s(15)).
* **Theorem 2.**  P = {(1, 1), (1, 1 + 1/√2), (1 + 1/√2, 1), (1 + 1/√2, 1 + 1/√2)} is unavoidable in
  [0, 2 + 1/√2]²:
  * corner cells by Lemma 1;
  * side strips by Lemma 2 with x = 1/√2, y = 1 (1/√2 + 2 < 2√2);
  * the central cell, split by a diagonal into two triangles with sides 1/√2, 1/√2, 1, by Lemma 3.
* The lower bound follows by scaling the packing up slightly (this handles touching closed squares), then
  pigeonhole: 5 squares on 4 points.

**Attribution.**  DS7 intro: "It is a little harder to show that s(5) = 2 + 1/√2 [7]" ([7] = Göbel).  DS7 says the
unavoidable-set technique is adapted from Stromquist.

* **Nagamochi Thm 1 applied to R₅ [I].**  For non-integer a, b ∈ (2, 3), ab − Δ(a) − Δ(b) = (a − 1)(b − 1) + 3.  So
  it would give "5 don't fit" when (a − 1)(b − 1) ≤ 2, e.g. a = b ≤ 1 + √2.  That is weaker than the true region, and
  Nagamochi's proof has a gap.
* **Conclusion:** there is one proof idea, Göbel's 4-point unavoidable set, with Friedman's lemmas the clean version of
  its "easy calculations".  No later paper reproves s(5) differently or as a special case of a general theorem.  The
  only rectangle-container theorems (Nagamochi, Karakuş) do not reach n = 5 with a ≥ 2.

**Stromquist 2003's lemmas** [read]: the extra flexibility that matters for rectangles.
* **Lemma 1 (corner, general).**  Let a, b ≤ 1.  A box with centre in [0, a] × [0, b] meets the x-axis, the y-axis,
  or the point (a, b).
  * This is for boxes in the open container.  chelokot's Lean corner lemma also takes any target with coordinates ≤ 1
    [read: doc].
* **Lemma 3 (side strip, his form of DS7 Lemma 2).**  If a + 2b ≤ 2√2, a centre in [0, a] × [0, b] forces the x-axis,
  (0, b) or (a, b).  Lowering the point row to distance b < 1 from the wall allows gaps up to 2√2 − 2b.
* **Lemma 4 (side strip with staggered points).**  This handles gaps a ∈ (2√2 − 2, 1), with the two points at
  different heights, (0, 1) and (a, b) with b ≤ f(a).  f is defined by a cubic in cos θ; f(0.894) ≈ 0.926,
  f(0.96) ≈ 0.769.
  * **This is the tool for a long-wall gap above 0.828**, which is exactly what R₅ needs near b = 3.

## 4. Extending to rectangles a × b, a ≥ 2 [I unless marked]

Set A = a (short), B = b (long).  Take points (p, q), (A − p, q), (p, B − q), (A − p, B − q) with p, q ≤ 1.  The central
cell is X × Y with X = A − 2p, Y = B − 2q.

1. **Corner cells:** fine for any p, q ≤ 1 (Stromquist Lemma 1; chelokot's general corner lemma).
2. **Side strips:** DS7 Lemma 2 needs X + 2q ≤ 2√2 (short walls) and Y + 2p ≤ 2√2 (long walls), plus X, Y ≤ 1.
3. **Central cell.**
   * The triangle lemma (or Göbel's inscribed disc) needs X² + Y² ≤ 1.  This circle touches the true boundary only at
     the square point X = Y = 1/√2.  So the published central lemmas prove s(5) and nothing else on the boundary.
   * **Centre-rectangle lemma (numerical, not in the literature):** a unit square whose centre lies in an X × Y
     rectangle contains a vertex iff **X + Y ≤ √2 and max(X, Y) ≤ 1**.
     * Grid probe (scratch, not kept): checks the worst case over centre and angle.  It is tight at
       45° on X + Y = √2 for X ∈ [0.45, 0.71], and axis-aligned at Y = 1 for X ≤ 0.414.
     * DS7 Lemma 4 (1 × 0.4) is a special case.
     * The threshold is exactly R₅'s boundary with p = q = 1 (X = a − 2, Y = b − 2):
       * Y ≤ 1 is the b = 3 piece;
       * X + Y ≤ √2 is the a + b = 4 + √2 piece.
     * Proof idea: the 45° extremal case in the style of DS7 Lemma 4.  Not yet written.
4. **Combined (linear feasibility in p, q; scratch script, not kept).**  With 1–3, the 4-point rectangle set proves
   the true boundary:
   * for a ≤ 3√2 − 2 ≈ 2.243 at b → 3 (forced: q = 1, Y = 1, p ≤ √2 − ½);
   * for a ≥ 4 − √2 ≈ 2.586 on a + b = 4 + √2 (forced: p = q = 1, so Y = b − 2 ≤ 2√2 − 2).

   In between it falls short by up to about 0.08 in b (at a = 2.40 it covers b < 2.920, against the truth 3).  The
   obstruction is a 45° square standing on the middle of the long wall between (1, 1) and (1, b − 1): Lemma 2's
   condition is sharp.
5. **Non-rectangular 4-point sets in the gap zone.**  These are Stromquist-Lemma-4-style staggered points.
   * Crude random local search over all 4-point sets, scored by the worst sampled escape (scratch script, not kept;
     1° angle grid, 60–90² centre grid; sampled escapes are lower bounds, so a positive value means avoidable):
     * at (a, b) = (2.45, 2.95), (2.40, 2.99), (2.55, 2.85), (2.35, 2.98), (2.45, 2.90), (2.40, 2.92), the best sets
       still had positive escape (0.001–0.018);
     * at (2.30, 2.95) and (2.50, 2.85) sets with negative sampled escape turned up.
   * This is weak evidence (local search, coarse grid) that **no 4-point unavoidable set reaches the boundary for
     a ≈ 2.35–2.55**.  There, a plain pigeonhole proof probably has to be replaced by one of:
     * a refined argument: a square avoiding all points must be the 45° wall-sitter, then a second-stage case analysis
       (as in Stromquist's s(10) proof, where "13 points, all denied to one block");
     * a weighted / measure certificate (our `CovMP` machinery).
6. **Formalisation reuse.**  chelokot's lemmas are exact-real Lean theorems and parameterised: corner target at
   coordinates ≤ 1, a side strip with row origin, height and gap, the triangle lemma, and Lemma 4 at arbitrary
   translation [read: doc].  They are what a Lean R₅ proof would need for pieces 4.3 (a ≤ 2.243) and 4.5 (a ≥ 2.586).
   * Do not import them (third-party, read-only): restate them as we did in `SpecChelokot.lean`.
   * Their scaling step (`lowerBound_succ_of_unavoidable`) is for square containers.  For rectangles, scale both
     sides by λ > 1 and stay inside the open region; R₅'s complement is star-shaped about 0 along the boundary pieces
     used.

## 6. Bottom line for the R₅ formalisation

* **Pieces 4.3 (2 ≤ a ≤ 2.243) and 4.5 (2.586 ≤ a ≤ 2 + 1/√2):** Göbel/Friedman 4-point pigeonhole with the points at
  (p, q)-insets.  Needs:
  * Friedman's corner and side lemmas (in Lean at chelokot);
  * one new lemma, the centre-rectangle lemma (X + Y ≤ √2, X, Y ≤ 1);
  * rectangle scaling.
* **Gap zone a ∈ (2.243, 2.586):** this covers the corner (1 + √2, 3) and part of both pieces.  No published tool
  covers it.  Stromquist's staggered-point Lemma 4 is the nearest idea, but a crude search suggests 4 points do not
  suffice.  Expect a second-stage argument or a measure certificate.
