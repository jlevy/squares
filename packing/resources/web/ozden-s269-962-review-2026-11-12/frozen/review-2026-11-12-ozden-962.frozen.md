## The Claim and What It Needs

The issue claims $s(269) \le C$ with $C = 16.899469075856$, the 12-place print of the
certificate’s side $S$, rounded up.
The certificate proves $s(269) \le S$: there are 269 closed unit squares, rotated
freely, with pairwise disjoint interiors inside the closed square $[0, S]^2$. The claim
follows when $C \ge S$, and directly when the same squares are accepted in $[0, C]^2$.

The hypotheses are six:

1. **The reading.** The envelope is `packing.squares:Witness/v2` with `representation:
   corners`, `scalar.kind: rational`, `square_size: '1'`, `origin: lower-left`,
   `axes: x-right-y-up`, `angle_unit: not-applicable`; every scalar is a canonical $p$
   or $p/q$ with $q > 1$ and $\gcd(p, q) = 1$; the JSON twin holds the same tree.
2. **The count.** 269 squares with ids 1 to 269 in order.
3. **Unit squares.** Each listed quadruple of corners is a unit square, listed in cyclic
   order.
4. **Containment.** Each square lies in $[0, S]^2$.
5. **Disjoint interiors.** No two squares’ interiors meet.
6. **The rotation sense.** The corners are read in the plane as given, $x$ to the right
   and $y$ up; control G6 shows that reading the rotation the other way changes the
   verdict.

`l117_check.py` decides the first two through the reader, and the next three twice.

- **Method A** (`l117_sat.py`, Python integers).
  Each square’s eight coordinates are written over their least common denominator $D$,
  as integer points $P_k$. The square is accepted when $U = P_1 - P_0$ has
  $U \cdot U = D^2$, $W = P_3 - P_0$ is $U$ turned a quarter either way, and
  $P_2 = P_0 + U + W$; its edge normals $u = U/D$ and $w = W/D$ are then exact rational
  unit vectors, and its own projections on them are intervals of length 1. A pair’s
  margin is the largest projection gap over the four normals of the two squares, its
  sign decided on integers and its value kept as an exact fraction: positive is apart,
  zero is contact with disjoint interiors, negative is overlap.
  Two convex polygons with disjoint interiors are weakly separated by a line parallel to
  an edge of one of them, so the three signs are exactly those three classes.
  A wall’s clearance is the least distance from a corner to it.
- **Method B** (`l117_hull.py`, `gmpy2.mpq`). A quadrilateral is a unit square when each
  of its four edges has squared length 1 and each of its four corners is a right angle
  (unit sides and right angles force opposite edges to be opposite vectors); its sense
  is the sign of its shoelace sum, $\pm 2$. A pair is decided from the finite set $V$ of
  the vertices of each square lying in the other, closed, and the crossing points of
  non-parallel edges. Every extreme point of the intersection of two closed convex
  polygons lies in $V$ (a boundary point inside two collinear edges is never extreme),
  and every point of $V$ lies in both, so the intersection is the convex hull of $V$.
  $V$ empty is apart; $V$ non-empty and collinear is contact, since the interior of an
  intersection is the intersection of the interiors; $V$ spanning the plane is overlap,
  and the hull’s area is the overlap area.
  A square lies in the box when its centre does and its interior meets none of four
  closed slabs of width 4 outside the walls, since a corner outside lies within
  $\sqrt{2}/2$ of a centre inside; each wall is classed by the same pair test against
  its slab.

The two share only the reader, which hands them integers.
The reader (`l117_reader.py`) refuses YAML anchors, aliases, explicit tags (the
non-specific `!` included), duplicate keys, several documents, and any resolved type but
string, integer, mapping and sequence, with integers in plain decimal form only; it
refuses witness keys outside the schema’s list and any layout it does not expect; it
requires the JSON twin, read with floats, constants and duplicate keys refused, to hold
exactly the same tree with exactly the same types.
The verdict is `ACCEPT` only when both methods accept every square, wall and pair, their
classes agree everywhere, and their listing senses agree.
