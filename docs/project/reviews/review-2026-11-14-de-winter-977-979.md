## The Claims and What They Need

Issue #977 claims $s(297) \le C_{297}$ with $C_{297} = 17.739455558289$, and #979 claims
$s(332) \le C_{332} = 18.739455558289$ and $s(409) \le C_{409} = 20.739455558289$, each
the 12-place print of its certificate’s side $S$, rounded up.
Each certificate proves $s(n) \le S$: there are $n$ closed unit squares, rotated freely,
with pairwise disjoint interiors inside the closed square $[0, S]^2$. A claim follows
when $C \ge S$, and directly when the same squares are accepted in $[0, C]^2$.

The format is the author’s own JSON: `n`, `side`, `square_size: "1"`, a `format` line,
and `squares`, each `[center_x, center_y, cos, sin]` as exact rationals, where
`(cos, sin)` is “the unit direction of one edge”.
The 297 file has a Witness/v2 twin with `representation: center-basis`,
`scalar.kind: rational`, `origin: lower-left`, `axes: x-right-y-up`. A square with
centre $C$ and edge direction $u = (c, s)$ is read as
$\{C + x u + y v : \lvert x\rvert, \lvert y\rvert \le 1/2\}$ with $v = (-s, c)$.

The hypotheses are six:

1. **The reading.** Each JSON file has exactly the keys above, `n` an integer, every
   scalar a canonical $p$ or $p/q$ with $q > 1$ and $\gcd(\lvert p\rvert, q) = 1$, and
   no floats, constants or duplicate keys.
   For 297 the twin is a valid Witness/v2 envelope with keys inside the schema’s lists
   and holds exactly the same $n$, side and squares.
2. **The count.** $n$ squares, ids 1 to $n$ in order (the twin’s ids; the JSON’s order).
3. **Unit squares.** Each $(c, s)$ has $c^2 + s^2 = 1$ exactly.
4. **Containment.** Each square lies in $[0, S]^2$. The JSON files do not say where the
   box is; the twin says the origin is the lower-left corner, and $[0, S]^2$ is the box
   under which all three files are accepted (F6).
5. **Disjoint interiors.** No two squares’ interiors meet.
6. **The rotation sense.** $(c, s)$ is read as an angle counterclockwise from the
   $x$-axis, $x$ to the right and $y$ up; control G6 shows that reading it the other way
   changes the verdict.

`l153_check.py` decides the first two through the reader, and the next three twice.

- **Method A** (`l153_sat.py`, Python integers).
  Each square’s centre is written over one denominator $E$, $C = (X, Y)/E$, and its edge
  direction over one denominator $r$, $u = (a, b)/r$. The square is accepted when
  $a^2 + b^2 = r^2$, which makes $u$ and $v$ exact rational unit vectors.
  For a pair and a unit normal $w$ of one of its squares, the projections on $w$ have
  half-lengths $1/2$ and $(\lvert u' \cdot w\rvert + \lvert v' \cdot w\rvert)/2$ for the
  other square, and the gap is the centre separation along $w$ less both: an exact
  Euclidean distance along $w$. All four gaps of a pair share the denominator
  $2 E_i E_j r_i r_j$, so the margin, their maximum, is decided on integers and kept as
  an exact fraction: positive is apart, zero is contact with disjoint interiors,
  negative is overlap.
  Two convex polygons with disjoint interiors are weakly separated by a line parallel to
  an edge of one of them, so the three signs are exactly those three classes.
  A wall’s clearance is the signed distance from the square’s extreme corner to it, from
  the half-extent $(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)/(2r)$.
- **Method B** (`l153_clip.py`, `gmpy2.mpq`). Each square is turned into its four
  corners $C \pm u/2 \pm v/2$ in cyclic order, and from then on only the corners are
  used. Four points are a unit square in cyclic order when all four sides have squared
  length 1 and both diagonals squared length 2: the converse of Pythagoras puts right
  angles at the second and fourth corners, which are then the two points at distance 1
  from both the first and the third.
  A pair is decided by clipping one square against the four closed half-planes to the
  left of the other’s directed edges, keeping boundary points; the result is the
  intersection exactly.
  Empty is apart; non-empty with zero area is contact, since the interior of an
  intersection of convex sets is the intersection of their interiors; positive area is
  overlap, by that area.
  Each wall is classed the same way, by clipping the square against the closed
  half-plane outside it.
  Containment is decided separately: a unit square lies in the box exactly when its
  intersection with the box has area exactly 1.

The two share only the reader (`l153_reader.py`), which hands them integers.
The reader refuses JSON floats, constants and duplicate keys, any key outside the
format’s five, a count other than `n`, and any scalar that is not canonical; for the
twin it refuses YAML anchors, aliases, explicit tags, duplicate keys, several documents,
any resolved type but string, integer, mapping and sequence, integers outside plain
decimal form, keys outside the schema’s lists, and any difference from the JSON file’s
$n$, side and squares.
The verdict is `ACCEPT` only when both methods accept every square, wall and pair and
their classes agree everywhere.
